# Client contract signing — briefing for Thai legal counsel

**Status:** engineering description complete; legal opinion outstanding
**Owner of the legal opinion:** in-country counsel (§28 pattern — Tilleke & Gibbins / Baker McKenzie / DFDL)
**Gate:** before the first tenant signs a contract with a client in production
**Engineering contact artefacts:** ADR-058 (mechanism), ADR-019 (credential service), §5.9.8 (threat model)

This document exists because ADR-058 states plainly that it "fixes the technical mechanism, not a legal
opinion". Nothing here interprets Thai law — that is counsel's work. What engineering can do is remove
every unknown on the technical side, so counsel is asked a narrow, answerable question instead of
"is our e-signature valid?".

Read §1–3 as the factual description of the system, §4 as the properties most likely to matter to the
opinion, and §5 as the questions we need answered.

## 1. What happens when a contract is signed

1. A contract is created in `DRAFT`. Its terms text and BOQ line snapshot are stored with it.
2. A PDF is attached — either uploaded, or generated by the platform from the stored terms and BOQ.
   The File Service computes and stores its **SHA-256** at rest.
3. **Contractor (internal) signature.** An authorised user (Tenant Admin / Executive / Project
   Manager) signs. The platform issues a W3C Verifiable Credential binding the signer to the
   document's SHA-256, signed with an Ed25519 key.
4. **Client (external) signature.** The contractor issues a single-use magic link addressed to a
   client name and email they supply. The client opens the link, and signs without holding a platform
   account. A second Verifiable Credential is issued, binding that client to the same SHA-256.
5. When both parties' signatures are recorded as `VERIFIED`, the contract moves to `SIGNED`, then
   `ACTIVE` on countersignature, and may later be `TERMINATED`. Each transition emits a domain event.

## 2. The evidence trail retained per signature

`finance.contract_signatures`, one row per signature:

| Field                 | Content                                                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `signer_party`        | `INTERNAL` or `CLIENT`                                                         |
| `signer_identity`     | internal: platform user id · client: the name and email the link was issued to |
| `document_hash`       | SHA-256 of the exact PDF at the moment of signing                              |
| `credential_ref`      | id of the Verifiable Credential carrying the cryptographic proof               |
| `signed_at`           | server timestamp (UTC)                                                         |
| `ip_address`          | source IP of the signing request                                               |
| `magic_link_token_id` | which single-use link was consumed (client signatures)                         |
| `verification_status` | `VERIFIED` / `PENDING` / `FAILED`                                              |

Supporting records: the signed PDF itself (File Service, with its stored hash), the issued credentials
(`credentials.verifiable_credentials`), and an append-only audit row per credential operation
(`credentials.audit_log` — the application role holds `SELECT`/`INSERT` only, no `UPDATE`/`DELETE`).

**What we can demonstrate for any given contract, on demand:** that the PDF presented today is
byte-identical to the one signed (hash comparison), that each signature's cryptographic proof verifies
independently of the platform, when each party signed and from what IP, and that no signature or audit
record was altered after the fact.

## 3. How verification works without us

Each signature's proof is an Ed25519 Data Integrity proof over the credential. Contractor-side worker
credentials resolve to a published issuer key document (`did:web`), and their revocation state is
published as a W3C Status List. A third party can therefore verify a signature offline, with no call
to Construction OS and no account. This is a deliberate design goal (ADR-019), and it is the property
that makes the evidence survive the platform.

## 4. Characteristics counsel should know before opining

These are stated plainly because they are the parts most likely to decide the answer. None is a defect;
each is a design choice that can be changed if the opinion requires it.

1. **Client identity assurance rests on the email address the contractor supplied.** Anyone who
   receives that link can sign. There is no ID document check, no phone OTP, and no prior enrolment of
   the client. The cryptography proves _the link was used and what was agreed to_; it does not
   independently prove _who the human was_.
2. **The link is single-use and expires after 7 days.** Consumption is recorded.
3. **The client signing key is ephemeral.** A fresh key is generated per signature and discarded; the
   public key is embedded in the credential. There is therefore no reusable client identity and nothing
   to steal later, but also no long-lived certificate bound to the client.
4. **Timestamps are our own server's.** There is no third-party timestamping authority.
5. **The contractor's issuer key** is generated by the platform and held encrypted (AES-256-GCM) with
   the master key in a secrets manager. It is not on a hardware security module, and it is not issued
   by a licensed certification authority.
6. **Retention** follows `docs/compliance/data-retention-policy.md`; signature rows and audit rows are
   never hard-deleted by the application.
7. **Data residency**: Thai-origin data stays in the Bangkok region per `data-residency-policy.md`.

## 5. Questions for counsel

1. Under the Thai Electronic Transactions Act (counsel to confirm the applicable act and amendments),
   what category does the mechanism in §1–3 fall into, and what does that imply for the burden of proof
   if a signature is disputed?
2. Does the identity-assurance model in §4.1 (email-delivered single-use link, no ID check) meet the
   standard for the contract types our tenants sign — main contracts, subcontracts, variation orders —
   or is a stronger method required for some of them, and which?
3. Is a licensed certification authority, or an ETDA-recognised service provider, required or merely
   advantageous for the contractor-side key in §4.5?
4. Does the absence of a third-party timestamp (§4.4) materially weaken the evidence, and would a
   qualified timestamping service change the answer?
5. What must be retained, in what form, and for how long, for a signed contract to remain admissible —
   and does our evidence set in §2 satisfy it?
6. Are there disclosure or consent steps that must be presented to the client _inside the signing flow_
   (for example, explicit consent to transact electronically) that we do not currently show?
7. Anything required specifically because a party is a consumer rather than a business?

## 6. Changes we can make if the opinion requires them

Listed so counsel can weigh remedies against effort, not as commitments:

- Phone OTP or ID-document capture before the client may sign (raises §4.1).
- A qualified third-party timestamp on each signature (§4.4).
- Keys held in an HSM/KMS, or certificates from a licensed CA (§4.5).
- Explicit in-flow consent and disclosure screens with their own recorded acceptance (§5.6).
- Longer or differently-scoped retention of the signed artefacts (§5.5).
